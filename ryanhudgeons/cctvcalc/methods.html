Storage and bandwidth

Bitrate. Unless you enter the camera's configured bitrate, each stream is estimated from a reference point of 4,000 kbps for 1080p (2.07 MP) H.264 at 30 fps in a typical scene. Bitrate grows with pixel count sublinearly, because larger frames compress better per pixel; an exponent of 0.8 reproduces the common vendor tables (about 2 Mbps at 1 MP, 6 Mbps at 4 MP, 12 Mbps at 8 MP).

kbps = 4000 × (MP ÷ 2.0736)^0.8 × (fps ÷ 30) × codec × scene
Codec factorScene factor
H.264 1.00 · H.264+ / smart 0.60 · H.265 0.55 · H.265+ / smart 0.30Low (indoor, static) 0.7 · Medium 1.0 · High (traffic, foliage, night noise) 1.4

Frame rate scales linearly, which is conservative for inter-frame codecs at high frame rates. Multi-sensor cameras multiply the stream by the sensor count.

Storage. Bytes per day = kbps × 1000 ÷ 8 × 86,400 × duty, where duty is 1 for continuous recording, the percentage you set for motion recording, or hours ÷ 24 for a schedule. Storage is shown in decimal terabytes, the unit drives are sold in; an NVR menu that shows TiB will read about 9% lower.

required TB = bytes/day × retention days ÷ 10¹²
drives = ceil( required × (1 + headroom) ÷ (drive size × (1 − overhead)) ), then RAID

RAID 1 mirrors (half the drives usable), RAID 5 spends one drive on parity (minimum 3), RAID 6 two (minimum 4), RAID 10 mirrors pairs (minimum 4). Days from installed divides the usable installed capacity by the bytes per day. Incoming bandwidth is the sum of the main streams, which flow whether or not they are being written; remote viewing assumes each viewer opens the full grid on sub-streams.

PoE budget

Power over Ethernet loses power in the cable as heat. The app solves the DC circuit for each device: the switch port pushes Vpse; the cable's loop resistance R drops it; the device draws its rated power P at whatever voltage reaches it.

V_pd = ( V_pse + √( V_pse² − 4 · P · R ) ) ÷ 2
I = P ÷ V_pd · loss = I² · R · port must source = P + loss

R = r × L for 802.3af/at (two pairs, two conductors in parallel each way) and r × L ÷ 2 for 802.3bt (four pairs), with r the conductor resistance per 100 m and L the run length. If the square root goes negative, the cable cannot deliver that power at all.

Classes 1–8 follow IEEE 802.3: the smallest class whose device allowance covers the draw. A device is flagged when the port would exceed its limit, when the device would see less than its minimum voltage, or when it sits within 10 percent of the port limit. Max run for power searches for the longest cable that keeps all three within limits, up to 500 m; Ethernet data still stops at 100 m (328 ft), which the app states separately. The switch's minimum voltage gives the conservative result; enter the real output (often 53–54 V) for a real-world figure.

UPS runtime = battery Wh × inverter efficiency × usable charge ÷ load, where the load is the PoE the switch sources ÷ 0.9 (conversion loss) plus the NVR and anything else on the UPS.

Lens and coverage

HFOV = 2 · atan( sensor width ÷ (2 · focal length) )
scene width = 2 · distance · tan( HFOV ÷ 2 )
pixels per meter = horizontal pixels ÷ scene width · pixels per foot = px/m × 0.3048

The datasheet's horizontal field of view is the accurate input; sensor widths (1/3″ 4.8 mm, 1/2.8″ 5.4 mm, 1/2.5″ 5.8 mm, 1/1.8″ 7.2 mm, 1/1.2″ 10.7 mm) are nominal and vary by model. DORI levels follow IEC 62676-4: Detect 25, Observe 62.5, Recognize 125, Identify 250 pixels per meter. The maximum distance for a level is pixels ÷ (2 · tan(HFOV ÷ 2) · px/m). Lens for a target runs it backwards: the scene width that carries the level at the distance, the field of view that gives it, and the focal length for the chosen sensor (f = sensor width × distance × px/m ÷ pixels).

Fiber loss budget

A fiber link loses light in the glass, at every mated connector pair and at every splice. The app adds the most each one may lose under TIA-568.3-D, so the result is the most a tested link should measure: real links usually come in lower.

link loss = length (km) × attenuation (dB/km) + connector pairs × loss per pair + splices × loss per splice + other
FiberMaximum cabled attenuation
OM1 / OM2 multimode3.5 dB/km at 850 nm, 1.5 dB/km at 1300 nm
OM3 / OM4 / OM5 multimode3.0 dB/km at 850 nm, 1.5 dB/km at 1300 nm
Single-mode, inside plant1.0 dB/km at 1310 and 1550 nm
Single-mode, outside plant0.5 dB/km at 1310 and 1550 nm

A mated connector pair is allowed 0.75 dB and a splice 0.3 dB. Every figure can be replaced with the cable's or the connector's datasheet value; the attenuation shows a Custom tag when it is. Lengths can be entered in feet, kilofeet, miles, meters or kilometers; the math runs in meters.

The optics check compares the link with the transceivers:

power budget = minimum transmit power (dBm) − receiver sensitivity (dBm)
margin left = power budget − link loss

The link works when the margin left is at or above the safety margin, 3 dB by default, a common allowance for aging and repairs. Between 0 and the safety margin it works with little to spare; below 0 there isn't enough light. The check doesn't look for the opposite problem, too much light on a short single-mode link overloading the receiver.

Sources: TIA-568.3-D attenuation as summarized on the Fluke Networks OM and OS poster; the 0.75 dB connector and 0.3 dB splice maximums from the FOA loss guidelines; the 3 dB aging margin from Rhys Haden on optical power budgets.

Coax loss

Coax loss rises with frequency and with length. The app uses the maximum attenuation of Series 59, 6 and 11 drop cable in ANSI/SCTE 74 2023, Table 11, at 68 °F (20 °C).

loss = attenuation at the frequency (dB/100 ft) × length ÷ 100 ft + splitter + other
level at the end = signal in − loss
margin = level at the end − minimum needed

The table lists 34 frequencies; the app uses 21 of them, every row up to 1002 MHz, then 1218, 1450, 1794, 2200 and 3000 MHz. Above 1 GHz the printed table has misprints (Series 6 reads 9.5 dB at 1800 MHz but 9.40 dB at 2000 MHz), so only the rows that rise smoothly are kept. Between rows the attenuation is interpolated on a log-log scale, and the frequency is held to 5–3000 MHz.

Speaker line 70/100 V

On a constant-voltage line each speaker's transformer tap sets the power it draws. The app adds the taps, sizes the amplifier, and works out what the wire loses.

total tap load = Σ tap × quantity
minimum amp = total ÷ max load · (80% by default: the amp rated 25% above the taps)
line load Z = V² ÷ total · (V² = 625, 5000 and 10,000 for 25, 70.7 and 100 V)
wire resistance R = 2 × run length × ρ ÷ area
line loss = 20 · log10( (Z + R) ÷ Z )
power reaching the speakers = ( Z ÷ (Z + R) )²
longest run at a loss limit L = Z × (10^(L/20) − 1) ÷ (2 × resistance per meter)

ρ is annealed copper at 20 °C, 100% IACS (1/58 Ω·mm²/m). An AWG size's area comes from its diameter, d = 0.127 mm × 92^((36 − n) ÷ 39); a metric size uses its nominal mm². The minimum amp is rounded up to a whole watt and the longest run down to a whole foot or meter, so neither reads past the true value.

Line loss of 0.5 dB or less is good practice; 1 dB is the most to accept. The thinnest wire shown is the thinnest size in the same family (AWG or mm²) that stays at 0.5 dB.

The calculation assumes every speaker sits at the far end of the run, the worst case. Speakers spread along the line lose less. Use the amplifier's rating at its 70 V or 100 V output, which can differ from its 4 or 8 Ω rating.

Sources: proav.de on 70 V / 100 V systems (Z = V² ÷ P; 1 dB maximum); Sound & Video Contractor on wire gauge (keep line loss below 0.5 dB); Pure Resonance Audio on amps and taps (load amps to 80% at most); ProAcoustics on choosing a 70 V amp (20% headroom).

The wire and the battery (the low-voltage tools)

Fire Alarm Battery & NAC, Access Control Power, Low-Z Audio and the notification tools share one wire table and one battery shape.

Wire. One conductor's resistance comes from NEC (NFPA 70) Chapter 9, Table 8, uncoated copper at 75 °C, for 18 to 10 AWG, solid or stranded; from IEC 60228's maximum resistance at 20 °C (class 1 solid, class 5 flexible) for mm² sizes; and from the app's copper formula for 22 and 20 AWG, which Table 8 doesn't list. Each figure moves to the tool's temperature with copper's coefficient:

R(T) = R_table × (1 + 0.00393 × (T − 20)) ÷ (1 + 0.00393 × (T_table − 20))
loop resistance = 2 × one-way length × R(T) · (out and back)
AWG, Ω per 1000 ft at 75 °C1816141210
solid7.77 confirm4.89 confirm3.071.931.21
stranded7.95 confirm4.99 confirm3.141.981.24

14, 12 and 10 AWG were confirmed in two sources; 18 and 16 AWG are the commonly published values, still to be checked in the code book. Fire alarm, access control, mass notification, school PA and intercom wire is figured at 75 °C, the life-safety basis; low-impedance audio stays at Speaker Line's 20 °C, so the same run reads a little more loss in the life-safety tools.

The voltage at the far end. The whole load sits at the end of the run, the worst case:

V_end = V_source − I × R_loop
longest one-way run = (V_source − V_min) ÷ I ÷ (2 × Ω per meter)

Standby battery.

required Ah = (standby A × standby hours + active A × active minutes ÷ 60) × (1 + safety factor ÷ 100)
battery = the smallest size on the list that covers it · (7, 12, 18, 26, 33, 40, 55 Ah; editable)

The safety factor is 25%, the load ÷ 0.8: since the 2022 edition, NFPA 72 has 80% of the battery's capacity carry the full load (the 2010 to 2019 editions used 20%) confirm the wording in the 2025 code book. A 24 V system takes two 12 V batteries of the size shown.

Figures beside a limit round toward the safe side: voltages and longest runs round down; drops, losses, amp-hours and supply ratings round up. 15.9915 V shows as 15.99 V, short of a 16.0 V minimum, never as 16.0 V.

Fire alarm battery and NAC

standby Ah = standby current × standby hours · 24 h
alarm Ah = alarm current × alarm minutes ÷ 60 · 5 min; 15 min for emergency voice/alarm communications
required = (standby Ah + alarm Ah) ÷ 0.8 · the 25% factor above

One device row at 0.25 A standby and 2.5 A in alarm needs 6.00 + 0.21 = 6.21 Ah, ÷ 0.8 = 7.76 Ah: a 12 Ah battery (the screen shows 7.77 Ah, rounded up).

The NAC check starts at the panel's output with a fully drained battery, 20.4 V on a 24 V panel, and holds the last appliance to its listed minimum operating voltage, 16.0 V for appliances listed 16–33 V; NFPA 72 has every appliance get at least its listed minimum, so both are inputs for the listed figures. For Class A the run is the whole loop, out and back. 2.0 A on 300 ft of 14 AWG at 75 °C: 1.842 Ω, a 3.68 V drop, 16.71 V at the last appliance, passing; 358 ft is the longest run on 14 AWG at that load. Appliances spread evenly along a circuit drop a little over half as much as the lumped worst case.

Access control power

always on = maglocks + fail-safe strikes + readers + other devices
unlocking = fail-secure strikes · (every one open at once: the peak)
peak = always on + unlocking · should stay at or under 80% of the supply's rating
supply needed = peak ÷ 0.8
battery Ah = ((always on + scheduled fail-secure strikes) × standby hours + other fail-secure strikes × unlocking minutes ÷ 60) ÷ 0.8

The standby level is UL 294's: Level I (none), II (30 min), III (2 h) or IV (4 h, the default). A door on an unlock schedule holds its fail-secure strikes unlocked for the whole standby time; the others count 5 minutes of unlocking (an input, not a code figure). Use a strike's inrush current where its datasheet lists one. 2 maglocks at 0.5 A, a fail-safe strike at 0.25 A, 3 fail-secure strikes at 0.45 A, 6 readers at 0.12 A and 4 request-to-exit sensors at 0.03 A peak at 3.44 A: 86% of a 4 A supply, over the 80% line, so 4.3 A or more; Level IV needs 10.59 Ah, a 12 Ah battery.

The lock is checked twice against its listed minimum voltage (90% of its rating unless its datasheet says otherwise): on the supply's rated output, which passes or fails, and at the battery's end voltage (85% of nominal: 10.2 V or 20.4 V), which is a caution, not a fail. A 0.5 A maglock on 150 ft of 18 AWG at 12 V gets 10.83 V on normal power, just over 10.8 V, and 9.03 V at the end of the battery: a fully drained 12 V supply can't hold a lock rated plus or minus 10% at any length. At 24 V a lock draws half the current and has twice the voltage to lose, so the same wire reaches about four times as far.

Low-Z audio 4–16 Ω

load Z = speaker impedance × speakers in series ÷ branches
loss at the speakers = 20 · log10( (Z + R) ÷ Z ) · the same formula as Speaker Line
power reaching the speakers = ( Z ÷ (Z + R) )²
heats the cable = R ÷ (Z + R) · a share of the amplifier's output
longest run at a target L = Z × (10^(L/20) − 1) ÷ (2 × resistance per meter)

A load at or above the amplifier's minimum (4 Ω unless its datasheet says otherwise) passes; one whose 80% (IEC 60268-5's floor for a speaker's impedance) falls under it gets a caution, since two 8 Ω speakers in parallel on a 4 Ω amplifier can dip to 3.2 Ω. Loss is graded as in Speaker Line: 0.5 dB or less good, 1 dB or less acceptable. An 8 Ω load at the end of 100 ft of 16 AWG at 20 °C loses 0.832 dB, 17.4% of the power, with 9.1% of the amplifier's output heating the cable (on screen, rounded up: 0.84 dB, 17.5%, 9.2%); 58 ft is the longest run at 0.5 dB. For long runs, use a 70 V or 100 V line.

Giant voice

level at d = rated − k × log2(d ÷ d_ref) − air absorption × d − excess attenuation
coverage radius: the distance where the level falls to the ambient + margin · (15 dB tones, 10 dB voice)
120 dBA distance: at most 6 dB per doubling, no absorption or excess attenuation
dB(C) to dBA: + (A − C weighting at the tone's frequency) · IEC 61672-1:2013

The fall-off is 10 dB per doubling of distance (FEMA CPG 1-17, 1980, and outdoor siren practice), 6 dB in an open field; air absorption (0, or about 5 dB per km at 1 kHz) and excess attenuation for terrain, buildings and trees (0–20 dB) are inputs. A 120 dB(C) array rated at 100 ft reaches 70 dB at 3,200 ft (FEMA's chart shows about 3,700 ft for suburban areas); in an open field, 32,253 ft. Arrays are often rated in dB(C) while the ambient and the limit are dBA: at 400 Hz the A − C difference is −4.8 dB, so that array is 115.2 dBA and reaches 70 dBA at 2,293 ft. The radius rounds down.

DoD allows no more than 120 dBA anywhere people may be, and the app keeps that limit on every pick as an input confirm. Near the array only spreading can be counted on, so the 120 dBA distance takes at most 6 dB per doubling and no absorption or excess attenuation: 200 ft around a 126 dB array rated at 100 ft, 105 ft around a 150 dB array rated at 1 m. It rounds up. The model is far-field; near the array, take the figures as a guide.

The site battery starts at the larger of NFPA 72 (2010)'s 7 days + 60 min confirm in the code book and UFC 4-021-01's 72 h + 60 min confirm the edition; the site sizes run past the cabinet sizes to 75, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300 and 400 Ah. 1.5 A standby and 12 A at full load need 330 Ah: a 400 Ah battery (UFC's 72 h: 150 Ah).

School PA

Each zone has its taps, a 25 V or 70.7 V line, the run to its last speaker and the wire. The zone's amplifier channel is its taps × 1.25, and its loss is mass notification's math at 75 °C, since the same lines carry emergency messages. 30 classroom speakers at 1 W on 25 V need 37.5 W and 20 hallway speakers at 2 W on 70.7 V need 50 W, 87.5 W in all; over 300 ft of 18 AWG the 25 V zone (20.8 Ω) loses 1.75 dB, too much (12 AWG is the thinnest within 0.5 dB), and the 70.7 V zone (125 Ω) 0.32 dB. On the DoD pick the thinnest wire offered starts at 16 AWG confirm. Call-in switches take 2-conductor shielded cable with the shield grounded at the head-end only, against the maker's run limit.

Speaker coverage

coverage diameter D = 2 × (ceiling − ear) × tan(dispersion ÷ 2)
spacing = D (edge to edge), 0.707 D (minimum overlap, the default), 0.5 D (center to center),
or (ceiling − ear) × 1.5 (±1.5 dB, speech), × 2.0 (±3 dB), × 2.5 (±6 dB)
speakers = ⌈length ÷ spacing⌉ × ⌈width ÷ spacing⌉
level = sensitivity (1 W, 1 m) + 10 · log10(watts) − 20 · log10(meters)
watts needed = 10^((target + headroom + edge loss − sensitivity + 20 · log10(meters)) ÷ 10)

Ears are at 5 ft standing or 3.5 ft seated (1.5 m, 1.1 m). The target is the ambient plus the margin (speech 10 to 15 dB, tones 15 dB), capped at 110 dBA confirm in the code book, with 6 dB of headroom. A lone speaker, or one spaced wider than minimum overlap, is about 6 dB down at the edge of its rated dispersion, so it needs four times the power there; in a grid at minimum overlap or closer, the neighbors make it up. 0.707 D is the usual figure for 1 ÷ √2 (0.7071) confirm. A 90° speaker in a 10 ft ceiling over standing ears covers 10 ft: 7.07 ft apart, 54 speakers in a 60 × 40 ft room; a 90 dB speaker at 1 W reads 86.3 dB 5 ft below, and 75 dBA with 6 dB of headroom takes 0.58 W at the edge of its circle (0.59 W on screen, rounded up).

Where the real world differs

  • Bitrates: night, rain and busy scenes push a camera above its daytime bitrate; the High scene factor or the configured bitrate covers it.
  • PoE: cold cable has slightly less resistance, hot cable more; switch ratings are marketing maximums; a damaged or poorly terminated run adds resistance the model cannot see.
  • Lenses: manufacturers round sensor sizes and fields of view; barrel distortion on wide lenses spreads pixels unevenly across the scene.
  • Fiber: dirty connectors are the most common cause of a link testing over budget; clean and inspect before blaming the cable. Bend radius and pinched cable add loss the model can't see.
  • Coax: the SCTE figures are at 68 °F (20 °C); attenuation rises with temperature, so a long run through a hot attic loses more than the app shows.
  • Speaker lines: the taps on real transformers are nominal, and the amplifier's 70 V or 100 V rating is the one that matters.
  • Fire alarm, access control and intercom runs: the whole load sits at the far end, the worst case; devices spread along a run drop less. A battery's real capacity falls with age and cold.
  • Life-safety figures: NFPA, UL, BS and EN texts are paywalled, so their figures come from public summaries; the ones marked confirm are the ones still to check in the code book, and the authority having jurisdiction has the final word.
  • Outdoor sound: wind, temperature layers and terrain move an array's reach from day to day; the model is far-field, so near the array its figures are a guide.
